# Cost & Loss Evidence — Indian Railways Maintenance-Block Optimisation

## Purpose

This document records cost, loss, resource-utilisation, and directly relevant operational evidence for the SIH26027 maintenance-block optimisation project.

The purpose is **not** to manufacture a universal Indian Railways price for every hour of possession or every minute of train delay. The purpose is to identify published evidence that establishes the operational problems the optimiser should address and, where possible, the economic consequences of those problems.

## Evidence rules

1. Every rupee figure must have a document behind it.
2. Cost, loss, asset value, revenue, and idle time are different quantities.
3. Foreign railway cost figures are not converted into Indian figures.
4. If a reliable Indian source does not establish a number, record **Not found** rather than estimating it.
5. Figures from audited samples or selected cases must not be presented as universal Indian Railways averages.
6. Model-generated values — synthetic costs, objective weights, buffers, penalties, etc. — must be labelled **model assumptions**, not Railway facts.

# 3. UK Schedule 4 — methodological precedent

## What is Schedule 4?

The UK's **Schedule 4 possessions regime** is a contractual mechanism in railway track-access agreements. When Network Rail takes temporary possession of the railway for planned engineering work and this disrupts train services, Network Rail makes payments to affected train operators.

The payments are intended to compensate operators for the **financial impact of planned disruption**, principally:

- revenue lost because fewer passengers travel or services cannot operate as planned; and
- additional operating costs caused by the disruption, such as replacement buses or changes to train operations.

The regime applies separately to passenger and freight operators, with different arrangements and payment mechanisms.

**Official sources:**

- Office of Rail and Road (ORR), material on Schedule 4 possessions.
- Network Rail, *Payments for planned disruption on the railway*.

## Why does Schedule 4 exist?

Schedule 4 has two important functions.

First, it protects train operators from financial risk caused by planned infrastructure possessions that are outside their direct control.

Second, it creates an **economic incentive to minimise disruption**. ORR describes the regime as a mechanism intended to incentivise Network Rail to plan possessions efficiently and minimise disruption to operators and final customers.

This creates a useful economic signal:

```text
Infrastructure work requires access to the railway
                    ↓
             Possession is taken
                    ↓
       Train services are disrupted
                    ↓
       Operator loses revenue and/or
          incurs additional costs
                    ↓
          Schedule 4 compensation
                    ↓
     Possession planning has a measurable
          financial consequence
```

The important lesson for SIH26027 is not the British compensation amount.

It is the **planning principle**:

> The economic impact of taking railway capacity away from train operations can be represented explicitly when planning maintenance possessions.

## What does it pay for?

For passenger operators, Schedule 4 arrangements can include:

1. **Revenue-loss compensation** — reflecting the financial effect of reduced revenue associated with disruption.
2. **Cost compensation** — reflecting additional costs caused by the possession, including items such as replacement buses and changes in train mileage.
3. For sufficiently large or sustained disruption, arrangements can allow claims based on actual costs and revenue losses rather than only a standard formula.

The amount can also depend on **how much advance notice** the operator receives. Earlier notification can reduce compensation because passengers have more opportunity to alter their travel plans, reducing the longer-term revenue impact.

Freight operators have a separate Schedule 4 arrangement.

## Why is this relevant to SIH26027?

Indian Railways is structurally different from the UK railway system, so the UK mechanism should **not** be copied directly.

The useful precedent is:

```text
Planned possession
       ↓
Train disruption
       ↓
Economic consequence
       ↓
Planning incentive
```

For SIH26027, the same principle can be represented internally through measurable disruption quantities:

- `train_delay_minutes`
- `delayed_NTKM`
- `machine_idle_hours`
- `maintenance_lateness`
- `unused_block_time`
- `schedule_change_penalty`
- `integrated_block_utilisation`

Because no nationally applicable Indian ₹/delay-minute or ₹/block-hour rate was identified, the prototype should use **explicitly labelled model shadow prices / weights** rather than claiming official Indian Railways costs.

### Critical distinction

**UK Schedule 4 is used as a methodological precedent only.**

Its monetary rates must **not** be converted into Indian rupees or inserted into the SIH26027 objective function.

# 4. Direct grounding of the SIH26027 objective function

The purpose of the evidence is to ground the **quantities being optimised**, rather than to manufacture unsupported rupee prices.

## Evidence → model mapping

| Published evidence | What it demonstrates | SIH26027 model quantity |
|---|---|---|
| 58,342 block-hours demanded vs 29,411 made available | Large gap between maintenance block demand and provision in the audited sample | `maintenance_block_shortfall` |
| 49.6% block shortfall | Maintenance access is a constrained planning resource | `maintenance_lateness` / unmet maintenance demand |
| 120,135 track-machine idle days | Maintenance assets spend substantial time unavailable for productive work | `machine_idle_hours` |
| 38,088 idle days (32%) because blocks were not given by Operating | Operating/block coordination directly contributes to machine idling | `machine_idle_hours_due_to_block` |
| 35,944 idle days (30%) because blocks were not planned by Divisions | Planning failures are a material source of machine idling | `machine_idle_hours_due_to_planning` |
| 2,383 h 36 min integrated-maintenance block shortfall | Separate departmental planning can fail to provide required integrated access | `unintegrated_block_time` |
| 27 h 35 min integrated corridor-block shortfall | Corridor-level coordination affects block provision | `integrated_block_utilisation` |
| Freight traffic measured in NTKM | Freight disruption can be represented as affected traffic exposure rather than an invented ₹/minute rate | `delayed_NTKM` |
| Documented derailment damages | Railway safety failures can have substantial financial consequences | Hard safety constraints / risk context |
| UK Schedule 4 | Planned possessions have measurable financial consequences for train operators and can create incentives for efficient possession planning | Justification for disruption shadow prices |

## Grounded objective

The model should minimise measurable operational disruption and maintenance inefficiency:

```text
Minimise:

    w_delay * total_train_delay_minutes
  + w_freight * delayed_NTKM
  + w_idle * machine_idle_hours
  + w_late * maintenance_lateness
  + w_waste * unused_block_time
  + w_change * schedule_changes

while maximising:

    w_integrated * integrated_block_utilisation
```

The coefficients are **model parameters**, not claims about official Indian Railways prices.

## Why not assign a rupee value to every term?

The evidence reviewed did not identify a reliable Indian government source establishing a nationally applicable:

- ₹ per maintenance-block hour;
- ₹ per train-delay minute; or
- ₹ per track-machine idle hour.

Accordingly, these values must not be invented.

A fabricated monetary rate would create false precision and make the optimisation appear more economically grounded than the evidence permits.

Instead, the prototype should expose these coefficients as **shadow prices / weights** and test the resulting schedules under multiple weight configurations.

# 5. Recommended sensitivity analysis

At minimum, evaluate four scenarios.

## Scenario A — Passenger-disruption priority

```text
High train-delay penalty
Medium freight penalty
Medium machine-idle penalty
```

## Scenario B — Freight priority

```text
High delayed-NTKM penalty
Medium train-delay penalty
Medium machine-idle penalty
```

## Scenario C — Maintenance priority

```text
High maintenance-lateness penalty
High machine-idle penalty
Medium train-disruption penalty
```

## Scenario D — Balanced

```text
Balanced disruption,
freight,
maintenance,
and possession-utilisation penalties
```

The purpose is not to claim that one set of weights represents an official Railway Board valuation.

The purpose is to demonstrate that the coordination mechanism remains effective when relative priorities change.

A stronger experiment should report:

- total train-delay minutes;
- delayed freight NTKM;
- machine idle hours;
- maintenance lateness;
- unused block time;
- number of shared/integrated blocks;
- number of separate possessions;
- objective value;
- CP-SAT optimality gap.

If the main conclusion remains stable across scenarios, the model is less dependent on any single arbitrary weighting choice.

# 6. Safety remains separate from economic weighting

Economic penalties must **never** be used as substitutes for railway safety constraints.

Examples of hard constraints include:

```text
No incompatible maintenance activities
on the same track at the same time.

No maintenance block may overlap
a train movement that requires the track.

Required possession duration must be respected.

Required safety buffers must be respected.

Required departmental/resource constraints
must be respected.

Required track/corridor access constraints
must be respected.
```

These are **hard CP-SAT constraints**.

The objective function decides which feasible plan is preferable; it does not decide whether an unsafe plan is acceptable.

# 8. Verification and rejection notes

## ₹32.96 crore is not a cost per derailment

The ₹32.96 crore figure is the total damages/loss of assets reported in the selected derailment cases analysed by CAG. It must not be divided by 1,129 and presented as an average derailment cost.

### Source inconsistency

CAG Report No. 22 of 2022 contains an internal inconsistency: the Executive Summary states **₹32.96 crore**, while the body text of para 3.2.2 states **₹33.67 crore** for the corresponding total. The Executive Summary also cites 24 factors responsible for derailments, while the body text cites 23.

This should be treated as an inconsistency in the primary document. Downstream presentations should either quote the exact section being referenced or avoid presenting either figure as a universal derailment cost.

## ₹5.16 crore is not a typical derailment cost

The ₹5.16 crore figure relates to one specific documented accident example in CAG Report No. 45 of 2017. It is evidence of an observed loss in that case, not a national average.

## Machine idle days are not rupee losses

The CAG finding of 120,135 idle machine-days demonstrates idle resource time. It does not establish the monetary value of each idle hour.

## Budget allocations are not block costs

Railway budget and safety/track-renewal allocations describe expenditure scale. They must not be divided by block hours to manufacture a block cost.

## Freight revenue is not train-delay loss

The ₹1,88,800 crore 2026-27 estimated freight revenue is a Railway revenue estimate. It does not imply a ₹/minute train-delay cost.

NTKM is an appropriate freight activity/exposure metric, but it is not itself a monetary loss.

# 10. Recommended final interpretation for SIH26027

The strongest defensible economic argument is:

> **CAG evidence demonstrates substantial maintenance-block shortfalls and significant track-machine idle time caused by block provision and planning failures. Railway traffic and freight statistics demonstrate the scale of traffic exposed to disruption. However, no nationally applicable Indian government source identified here establishes a universal ₹/block-hour, ₹/delay-minute, or ₹/machine-idle-hour rate. Therefore, SIH26027 does not invent such figures. Instead, the optimiser directly minimises measurable operational impacts and uses explicitly labelled shadow prices/weights, with sensitivity analysis to test robustness.**

This produces a clean separation:

1. **Railway fact** — supported by CAG, Railway/official documents, Parliament, PIB, or other authoritative sources.
2. **Model assumption** — explicitly chosen for the prototype and tested through sensitivity analysis.
3. **Optimisation result** — produced by CP-SAT for a defined scenario.

Only category 1 should be presented as an observed Indian Railways statistic.

---

# 11. Final verdict

No reliable primary Indian source checked establishes a universal:

- ₹/maintenance-block-hour;
- ₹/train-delay-minute; or
- ₹/track-machine-idle-hour.

Therefore these should **not** be used as factual Indian Railways cost parameters.

The strongest defensible evidence is instead:

- documented maintenance-block shortages;
- documented machine-idle time and its causes;
- documented integrated-block shortfalls;
- documented maintenance-block durations;
- documented derailment damages in selected cases;
- documented Railway budget and maintenance expenditure;
- freight NTKM/revenue as operational context;
- Railway planning horizons and network-capacity pressure; and
- the UK Schedule 4 mechanism as a methodological precedent for explicitly recognising the economic impact of planned possessions.

**Core design principle:**

> **ML predicts maintenance duration and urgency. CP-SAT decides the schedule. Published railway evidence grounds the measurable objectives. Hard safety constraints remain non-negotiable. Shadow prices are model assumptions, not invented Railway facts.**
