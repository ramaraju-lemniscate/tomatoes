# SIH26027 Technical Working Document: AI-Powered Automatic Block Planning for Indian Railways

*A build-and-strategy handbook for a 2nd-year B.Tech ICT team, Smart India Hackathon 2026 (Ministry of Railways, Software, deadline 20 September 2026, no dataset supplied)*

## TL;DR
- **This is fundamentally a resource-constrained possession-scheduling problem, not a machine-learning problem.** The right architecture is a two-layer "estimate-then-optimise" pipeline: ML predicts task durations, degradation and criticality (the *estimation* layer); a constraint/integer optimiser (Google OR-Tools CP-SAT) decides which maintenance blocks to grant, when, and merged across departments (the *decision* layer). Present this framing to judges explicitly — it is defensible and is exactly how ProRail (Netherlands), Trafikverket (Sweden) and Network Rail (UK) actually do it.
- **The domain is winnable on credibility.** Judges are railway operations professionals; the team must fluently use block/corridor/possession/disconnection vocabulary, anchor synthetic data to published IRPWM/USFD/AC-Traction-Manual periodicities, and lead with the real, audited pain point: the CAG found a ~50% shortfall of maintenance blocks against engineering demand and ~33% of granted block time unused by track machines. Reproduce that number as the "before" baseline your system improves.
- **Genuine novelty is available in the multi-department (Engineering + S&T + Traction) block-merging problem in the Indian corridor-block context** — the OR literature overwhelmingly treats single-department track possession. Solve the coordinated merge with a shared "possession window" that amortises setup/withdrawal time across departments, and you have a real contribution, not a reimplementation.

## Key Findings

### 1. What the problem actually is
SIH26027 asks you to replace decentralised, manual maintenance-block requests (each department independently demanding time on the track via BDMS) with a single coordinated optimiser that ingests defects/overdue tasks from TMS, SMMS and TDMS plus corridor/timetable availability from the COA, and produces optimised weekly and monthly block schedules that maximise asset availability. Stripped of the buzzwords, this is the **railway infrastructure maintenance possession-scheduling problem** — a well-studied operations-research problem — with an Indian-specific multi-department coordination twist. Recognising this lets you stand on 20 years of published OR rather than inventing from scratch.

### 2. The domain (this is what wins or loses the pitch)

**Blocks and their types.** A "block" in Indian Railways is a planned period during which a section of track is taken out of normal traffic use so maintenance can be performed safely. Types the team must distinguish:
- **Traffic block / line block / possession:** all train traffic barred on the section; requested by the engineering official in charge and granted at Divisional Railway Manager (DRM) level, with the adjacent Station Master implementing it and (on busy sections) the Section Controller deciding exact timing. Permanent-way staff "take possession" of the track.
- **Power block:** electric traction barred for OHE (overhead equipment) work; the Traction Power Controller (TPC) coordinates shutting down the catenary supply. A *programmed power block* is scheduled routine OHE maintenance; an *emergency power block* follows OHE damage.
- **Power-and-traffic block:** bars all traffic (not just electric) for OHE work.
- **Disconnection:** the "D" in BDMS — taking signalling gear (points, interlocking) out of service for S&T work, coordinated with the Station Master/controller.
- **Corridor block / maintenance corridor:** a pre-planned, timetabled daily window (Indian Railways moved to a ~3-hour daily maintenance-corridor policy — see below) reserved for maintenance so it need not be negotiated ad hoc.
- **Mega block / jumbo block:** long consolidated blocks (several hours up to 60+ hours) typically on Mumbai suburban lines — "mega block" on Central Railway, "jumbo block" on Western Railway — run mostly on Sundays; e.g. a 63-hour mega block was taken at Thane to widen platforms.
- **Non-interlocked working:** temporary operation with signalling interlocking suspended, used during major S&T/yard remodelling.

**BDMS (Block & Disconnection Management System).** Developed by CRIS (Centre for Railway Information Systems), BDMS is described as "a unified platform for real-time processing and monitoring of traffic, power, and disconnection blocks across various railway departments," accessed via the TDMS login and rolled out first in Central Railway. It digitises the request→approval→grant workflow, but **planning across departments is still done independently and manually — which is exactly the gap SIH26027 targets.** The generic workflow BDMS digitises: an Engineering official (typically the SSE/P.Way) or S&T official (JE/Signal) raises a block demand → Operating (Section Controller / Divisional control) coordinates it against train paths → grant is authorised at DRM level → the Station Master implements it on the ground, issuing a **caution order** to the last train before the block and setting block instruments to "line blocked" until the block is withdrawn. *(Caveat: the exact BDMS screens, field-by-field approval chain and statutory lead times could not be confirmed from a primary CRIS manual; this chain is reconstructed from CRIS summaries and the long-standing IRFCA operations reference.)*

**Source systems (what data each holds).**
- **TMS (Track Management System):** the civil-engineering track asset register — inspections, Track Recording Car (TRC) geometry runs, USFD results, rail/weld fractures, defect severity, and an alerting chain from Junior Engineer up to Railway Board; launched network-wide in 2020 and hosted on the IRCEP portal (ircep.gov.in).
- **SMMS (Signalling Maintenance & Management System):** the S&T asset register with geotagged assets, maintenance schedules, overdue-maintenance reports, "codal-life-outlived" reports and signalling-asset-failure reports; web + mobile app by CRIS, built around the Signal Engineering Manual (SEM).
- **TDMS (Traction Distribution Management System):** OHE, PSI (Power Supply Installation), RCC, TPC and Tower Wagon KPIs; GPS foot-patrolling with real-time OHE-defect capture and photos, real-time breakdown logging, and OHE power-block requisition with SMS alert to the TPC.
- **COA (Control Office Application):** manages block corridor availability, live train running and control charts — the authoritative source of the timetable and path availability your optimiser must respect.

**Maintenance periodicity norms (anchor synthetic data to these).**
- **USFD (Ultrasonic Flaw Detection) of rails**, per the RDSO *Manual for Ultrasonic Testing of Rails and Welds* (Revised 2012), "need-based concept": one test per ~8 GMT (gross million tonnes) of traffic, with calendar periodicity varying by sectional GMT — approximately GMT >8≤12 → 12 months; >12≤16 → 6 months; >16≤24 → 4 months; >24≤40 → 3 months; >40≤60 → 2 months; >60≤80 → 1.5 months; >80 → 1 month, subject to a maximum interval of one year. Welds get a First Periodic Test after ~1 year, then every 40 GMT.
- **Tamping / track machines**, per the IRTMM (Indian Railways Track Machine Manual) and IRPWM Para 226: track machines should ideally get a single block of **at least 4 hours per day, or two blocks of 2.5 hours each**, for effective working.
- **Track Recording Car (TRC) runs:** fortnightly for routes ≥130 km/h, monthly for others.
- **OHE / traction:** governed by the *Indian Railways Manual of AC Traction Maintenance and Operation* (1994), Vol II Part I (Fixed Installations), which sets OHE, switching-station and other periodicities.
- **Signalling:** the SEM and standard signalling-gear schedules use maintainer/JE/SSE tiers with fortnightly (F), monthly (M), quarterly (Q), half-yearly (HY) and yearly (Y) checks per gear item (block instruments, point machines, rodding runs, etc.).

**Corridor-block policy.** Indian Railways took a decision in **2018** that every section would keep its tracks vacant for **at least three hours daily for maintenance**, implemented via the "zero-based timetable" as a dedicated maintenance corridor whose exact timing varies by division/zone. This was stated on the record by Railways Minister **Ashwini Vaishnaw in the Rajya Sabha (17–19 March 2025)**: a "tough decision" was taken in 2018 that "in every section, the railway tracks would be kept vacant for at least three hours for maintenance," a decision he said followed a **detailed study by IIT Bombay** (whose team included Prof. Madhu Belur, Electrical Engineering, and Prof. Narayan Rangaraj, IEOR, working with CRIS and the Zonal Railways). The 2018 policy was publicly previewed in 2020 by then-Railway Board Chairman V.K. Yadav as a "fixed three-hour" maintenance corridor in the new zero-based timetable. A 2016 Railway Board Track Directorate document on "Fixed-Time Integrated Corridor Blocks" appears to have formalised the corridor-block concept earlier. *(Caveat: the exact circular number/date of the 2016 document could not be verified — the source PDF link is dead.)*

**The pain point, quantified (your "before" baseline — this is gold in a pitch).**
- The **CAG compliance audit "Maintenance of track on heavy traffic sections over Indian Railways" (Report No. 45 of 2017, laid in Parliament 13 March 2018)** found a shortage of **about 50% of blocks against engineering demand** across the selected divisions — **58,342 hours demanded vs 29,411 hours made available (49.6% shortfall)** — with individual divisions ranging from ~17% (SR Chennai) to ~72% (SER Chakradharpur) shortfall. The audit press release states plainly that "the block demanded by Engineering Department for maintenance works were not fully made available, and where made available, these were not optimally utilized." On average **33% of granted block time could not be utilised** by track machines, and **46% of lost block time was attributable to travelling/shifting of machines**. NCR Allahabad received corridor blocks of only 90–120 minutes — below the 2.5-hour norm.
- The later **CAG performance audit "Derailment in Indian Railways" (Report No. 22 of 2022, tabled 21 December 2022)** found track-machine idling was caused by **blocks not given by the Operating Department (32%), blocks not planned by Divisions (30%)**, operational problems (19%), non-availability of staff (5%) and no scope of work (3%) — i.e. **~62% of idling tied directly to block-planning failures.** It also attributed 422 derailments (April 2017–March 2021) to the Engineering Department, with "maintenance of track" the leading factor.
- The Minister has publicly claimed rail fractures fell from **2,500 in 2013-14 to ~250 in 2024 (a ~91% reduction)** as a result of the corridor policy; present this as an official claim, not an independently audited fact.

**Essential vocabulary to use fluently:** block, corridor, possession, path, disconnection, section, block section, TSR (temporary speed restriction), caution order, line capacity, sectional running time, GMT (gross million tonnes), zone/division, DRM, Section Controller, TPC, SSE/P.Way, JE/Signal, mega block, engineering allowance, traffic recovery time.

**Real operational constraints to model:**
- Minimum useful block duration (the 4-hour tamping block per IRPWM; blocks under ~90–120 min are of limited value — the CAG explicitly flagged 90–120-min corridor blocks as below the 2.5-hour norm).
- Travel/setup/withdrawal time for gangs and machines (the CAG's 46% travel-loss figure justifies modelling this explicitly).
- Safety protocols (the "One Minute Safety Drill" before every block; caution orders; protection of the block).
- Engineering hours vs traffic hours (blocks compete directly with train paths).
- Interlocking constraints (disconnections affect adjacent signalling).
- Rail-temperature windows for LWR/CWR (long-welded/continuously-welded rail) tamping — regular maintenance confined to specified rail-temperature ranges and completed before summer.

### 3. The OR literature (what to read and cite)
- **Surveys:** Tomas Lidén, *"Railway Infrastructure Maintenance — A Survey of Planning Problems and Conducted Research," Transportation Research Procedia* 10 (2015): 574–583 — the canonical survey; classifies problems as strategic/tactical/operational and catalogues possession-time-per-activity. Sedghi et al. (2021) is a more recent decision-support-model review.
- **Foundational formulation:** Budai, Huisman & Dekker, *"Scheduling preventive railway maintenance activities," Journal of the Operational Research Society* 57(9) (2006): 1035–1044 — defines the **Preventive Maintenance Scheduling Problem (PMSP)**: short routine activities plus long unique projects scheduled *together* to minimise possession + maintenance cost, with fixed-interval and max-interval variants and an explicit end-of-horizon term. This is the closest textbook analogue to SIH26027 — base your model on it. (The full 2004 working-paper version, Econometric Institute Report EI 2004-41, is free from the Erasmus University repository.) The eight-phase Dutch maintenance-planning process it documents — budget → quality prediction → project definition → prioritisation → possession allocation/timetabling → project combination → short-term scheduling → feedback — is a ready-made process diagram for your slides.
- **Integrated timetabling + maintenance (state of the art):** the **INFORMS RAS Problem Solving Competition 2016** spawned MILP models integrating train timetabling and track-maintenance-task scheduling at the block-section (microscopic) level — decision variables are the entrance/exit times of trains per block section plus the start/end of each maintenance task; objective minimises total train travel time plus maintenance tardiness; because the integrated problem is strongly NP-hard, an iterative algorithm computes near-optimal solutions. D'Ariano and colleagues' stochastic tactical integration (using the RAS 2012/2016 instances, solved in CPLEX) is a good reference for bi-objective robust formulations.
- **National-scale planning:** ProRail's annual maintenance schedule is built more than 2 years ahead; Oudshoorn et al. (2022, *Computer-Aided Civil and Infrastructure Engineering*) solve a national-level planned-maintenance MILP that beats the schedules then in use — evidence these methods work at scale. Dutch NS/ProRail work (van Aken et al. 2017) on the Train Timetable Adjustment Problem under possessions uses an extended Periodic Event Scheduling Problem (PESP) MILP with retiming/reordering/short-turning/cancellation and row generation for station-capacity constraints; note their striking real-world statistic — NS planners needed ~14 weeks to hand-produce a single feasible day-timetable under possessions, which is the manual bottleneck your automation attacks.
- **European practice references:** ProRail event-request MILP minimising passenger delay; Trafikverket opportunistic tamping (Khosravi et al., *Heliyon* 2024 — MILP + genetic algorithm on the Main Western Line, showing a 3-month tamping interval yields >50% fewer corrective interventions than a 9-month interval); a recent MaxSAT model for the Deutsche Bahn network possession problem; and Lidén & Joborn's coordinated maintenance-window/train-path MILP (the key "combined approach").
- **Indian-Railways-specific published OR:** thin. There is a multi-agent Rural Postman Problem with temporal unavailability for inspecting Indian track without disturbing trains (arXiv 2101.04950) and general IR-digitisation papers, but essentially **no published block-optimisation work for IR — this is where your novelty lives.**

**Standard mathematical formulations.**
- *Time-indexed MILP* is the workhorse: binary `x[task, slot] = 1` if a task is assigned to that time slot. Constraint families: (a) each task scheduled within its due window; (b) possession/track-occupation exclusivity per section per slot; (c) resource/crew/machine capacity (cumulative); (d) grouping/combinability bonuses for co-scheduling; (e) timetable-conflict (no maintenance block overlapping a scheduled train path on that section). Objective combines possession cost, tardiness/urgency penalty and criticality-weighted downtime.
- *Constraint-programming formulations* (interval variables + no-overlap + cumulative) are usually more natural and faster for the pure scheduling structure.
- Early Indian-relevant work exists too: Cheung et al. (1999) solved railway track-possession assignment as a constraint-satisfaction problem — useful precedent that CP fits this problem.

**Computational difficulties and how the literature handles them.** Combinatorial explosion with fine time discretisation; symmetry (interchangeable slots/identical tasks) that stalls branch-and-bound; the granularity tradeoff (coarse slots → small model, crude schedules; fine slots → realistic, exponential). Standard remedies: spatial/temporal aggregation (a macroscopic pass before microscopic conflict resolution), rolling-horizon decomposition, column generation / row generation, and metaheuristics (genetic algorithms, simulated annealing, large-neighbourhood search) for large instances.

**Legitimate role of ML.** Predicting maintenance-task durations; track-geometry degradation modelling and remaining-useful-life (RUL) prediction (Kalman-filter and ML approaches on longitudinal level / twist; a Portuguese-network multiple-linear-regression model built on 2,819 tamping defects across 66,892 km predicts post-tamping geometry); failure-risk prediction from defect data; and criticality scoring. **ML feeds parameters into the optimiser; it does not "schedule."**

### 4. Technical tooling
- **Google OR-Tools CP-SAT is the recommended solver.** Model each maintenance task as an interval via `NewIntervalVar` / `NewOptionalIntervalVar` (the latter carries a presence literal); enforce single-occupation of a track section with `AddNoOverlap`; enforce crew/machine capacity with `AddCumulative`; choose which section/gang via the "alternative resources / optional intervals" pattern (one optional interval per task-resource pair, exactly one present); set objectives with `Minimize`/`Maximize`. CP-SAT natively understands optional intervals — no-overlap and cumulative correctly ignore absent intervals and can even force a presence literal false when a task cannot fit. Work directly from the official `ortools/sat/docs/scheduling.md` (it covers intervals, optional intervals, NoOverlap, Cumulative, alternative resources, ranking, and RCPSP with worked Python samples).
- **CP-SAT vs MILP — when each wins.** For pure disjunctive/cumulative scheduling with all-integer data, CP-SAT is usually as good or better than open-source MILP (CBC/HiGHS) and competitive with commercial CP Optimizer; it won the MiniZinc gold medals for years. Use MILP (PuLP/HiGHS/CBC, or Gurobi if available) when you have genuinely *continuous* variables or a strongly linear cost structure — benchmarks show Gurobi can slightly edge CP-SAT on pure linear and some grouped-periodic-scheduling instances, while CP-SAT dominates on complex combinatorial constraints. **Recommendation: prototype in CP-SAT; keep a small PuLP/HiGHS MILP as a cross-check to validate the model on tiny instances.** Note CP-SAT requires integer data (scale non-integers by a constant).
- **Time discretisation.** Choose slot granularity deliberately — e.g. 15- or 30-minute slots for the weekly plan; hourly or half-day for the monthly. Document the tradeoff. Prefer interval variables over a fully time-indexed binary formulation where you can, to avoid model blow-up.
- **Honest optimality-gap reporting.** After `solver.Solve(model)`, check the status (`OPTIMAL`, `FEASIBLE`, `INFEASIBLE`, `MODEL_INVALID`, `UNKNOWN`); report `solver.ObjectiveValue()` (the incumbent) and `solver.BestObjectiveBound()` (the proven bound); the gap is (objective − bound)/bound. Under a time limit you will usually get `FEASIBLE` with a nonzero gap — say so plainly ("within X% of a proven bound in N seconds"). Beware: presolve edge cases can report `OPTIMAL` with a stale bound, so always sanity-check. Judges respect an honest gap far more than a claimed "optimal" that is really a timed-out incumbent. The "CP-SAT Primer" by Dominik Krupke (its "Understanding the Log" and "Parameters" pages) explains the log, `gap_integral`, and status semantics precisely.

### 5. Data
- **Publicly available (real):** the Indian Railways train timetable on **data.gov.in** (train-wise arrival/departure per station, route, distance); the **`datameet/railways` GitHub repo** (GeoJSON of stations and train line-strings with station codes and zones — usable to reconstruct partial section/network structure); several Kaggle timetable dumps; and **NTES / RailRadar** for live running status. There is **no public dataset of maintenance defects, blocks, or possessions** — consistent with the PS supplying none.
- **Synthetic maintenance data (must survive railway-engineer scrutiny).** Anchor every generated task to a published periodicity: generate USFD tasks at the GMT-based frequencies above; tamping tasks at IRPWM intervals with the 4-hour block need; OHE tasks per the AC Traction Manual; signalling tasks per the F/M/Q/HY/Y schedule. Give each task: a due date = last-done date + periodicity; a criticality tag (safety-critical vs routine, mapped to Indian defect classes such as IMR — Imminent-failure Marked for Removal); a required possession type (traffic / power / disconnection); a section; and a duration distribution. Use a **real section's timetable** (from data.gov.in) as the corridor-availability input so blocks genuinely compete with real train paths. **Reproduce the CAG ~50%-shortfall scenario as your baseline, then show your optimiser's improvement** — this is the single most persuasive evaluation you can run.

## Details

### The hard sub-problems the team must solve themselves
1. **Decision variables.** Choose between time-indexed binaries `x[task, slot]` and interval variables `(start, duration, end, presence)`. Interval variables in CP-SAT are cleaner for no-overlap; time-indexed is easier to couple with a fixed timetable grid. This is the single most important modelling decision — make it consciously and be able to defend it.
2. **Timetable-conflict constraint.** How do you express "no maintenance block on section S during a slot occupied by a scheduled train path"? Two options: (i) pre-compute per-section *free windows* from the timetable and constrain maintenance intervals to lie inside them (simpler, macroscopic — realistic for weekly/monthly planning and far more tractable); or (ii) model train paths as fixed intervals and no-overlap them against maintenance intervals (microscopic, à la INFORMS RAS). Start with (i).
3. **Multi-department block merging — the core novelty.** Model a shared *possession window* on a section: when Engineering, S&T and Traction tasks are co-scheduled in one window, the fixed setup/withdrawal/protection cost is paid **once**, not three times. Introduce a variable "a possession is opened on section S at time T," link department tasks to it, and correctly handle that some tasks need a *power* block (OHE dead) while others only need a *traffic* block — you cannot merge a live-OHE task into a power-block window, and vice-versa.
4. **Criticality vs disruption.** Weight doing an urgent, overdue, safety-critical task now (cancelling valuable train paths) against deferring it. This is multi-objective: use a weighted sum with defensible weights, or lexicographic (safety first), or produce a Pareto front. Be ready to justify the weights.
5. **Weekly vs monthly horizon.** Long tasks and rare heavy-machine campaigns belong in the monthly plan; short routine tasks fit the weekly. Decide between hierarchical solving (monthly fixes big possessions, weekly fills detail — this mirrors real ProRail practice and is more tractable) and a joint rolling-horizon model.
6. **Setup/withdrawal and machine travel time.** Model machine-shifting time explicitly — the CAG 46%-travel-loss figure makes this an easy credibility win.
7. **Infeasibility handling.** When demand exceeds available block hours (the realistic case), the model must gracefully *defer* lowest-priority tasks rather than return `INFEASIBLE`. Use soft constraints with penalties, not hard deadlines.

### Insights to articulate to judges
- The bottleneck is not computing a schedule; it is **coordinating three departments' competing demands against scarce, timetable-constrained block windows.** Your system's value is coordination + prioritisation, quantified against the CAG baseline.
- ML and optimisation play distinct, complementary roles; conflating them (e.g. "an LSTM that outputs a schedule") is exactly the naive approach to avoid.
- Merging blocks across departments delivers the biggest asset-availability gains, because setup/withdrawal overhead dominates short blocks.

### Where the novelty is (real contribution vs reimplementation)
- **Multi-department (Engineering + S&T + Traction) coordinated block merging in the Indian corridor-block regime** — under-served in a literature dominated by single-department track possession.
- Integrating the Indian **~3-hour daily maintenance-corridor policy** as a hard structural constraint.
- A **criticality score grounded in Indian defect classifications** (IMR rail marking, USFD defect classes) feeding the optimiser.
- **Reproducing and beating the CAG-documented ~50% block-shortfall baseline** as a rigorous evaluation protocol — almost no student project defines its "before" state from a national audit.

### Known failure modes / pre-mortem
- **Building a CRUD dashboard, not an optimiser.** The PS is ~80% modelling; a polished React frontend with a greedy heuristic behind it loses to a rougher UI with a real CP-SAT model. Weight effort accordingly.
- **Fake "AI."** Bolting on a neural net that does nothing useful. Use ML only where it genuinely estimates a parameter.
- **Synthetic data a railway engineer laughs at.** If periodicities/durations aren't anchored to IRPWM/USFD/AC-Traction norms, credibility evaporates instantly.
- **Claiming "optimal" when you timed out.** Report gaps honestly.
- **Ignoring the timetable.** A block plan that doesn't respect real train paths is meaningless.
- **Model too big to solve.** Over-fine discretisation → no solution in demo time. Start coarse, refine later.
- **No baseline.** Without a "before" (manual/greedy) to compare against, you cannot demonstrate improvement.

### 8-week build plan (modelling-weighted, not frontend-weighted)
- **Week 1 — Domain immersion.** Read the Lidén 2015 survey + Budai/Huisman/Dekker 2006. Learn block/possession vocabulary. Skim IRPWM Ch. 6, the USFD Manual, the AC Traction Manual, and CAG Report 45 of 2017. Deliverable: a one-page glossary + problem restatement in railway language.
- **Week 2 — CP-SAT fundamentals.** Work through the official `scheduling.md` (interval vars, NoOverlap, Cumulative, optional intervals, job-shop, RCPSP). Build a toy single-section, single-department block scheduler.
- **Week 3 — Data.** Pull a real section's timetable from data.gov.in / the datameet GeoJSON. Build the synthetic maintenance-task generator anchored to periodicity norms. Define the data schema mirroring TMS/SMMS/TDMS fields.
- **Week 4 — Core single-department model.** Tasks as optional intervals; no-overlap per section; cumulative crew capacity; due-window and timetable-free-window constraints; criticality-weighted objective. Report optimality gaps from day one.
- **Week 5 — Multi-department merging (the novel core).** Shared possession windows; setup/withdrawal amortisation; power-vs-traffic block typing. Spend real time here.
- **Week 6 — Horizons + ML layer.** Weekly/monthly via hierarchical decomposition and rolling horizon. Add a simple, honest ML estimation layer (gradient-boosted regression for duration/criticality on synthetic history is fine if framed transparently).
- **Week 7 — Evaluation + visualisation.** Reproduce the CAG ~50%-shortfall baseline and a greedy baseline; measure asset-availability uplift, block-hours utilised, tasks-completed-on-time, average tardiness. Build a lean Gantt-of-blocks (by section/department) with before/after KPIs.
- **Week 8 — Harden and pitch.** Freeze scope; write the pitch around the two-layer framing and the CAG numbers; rehearse domain Q&A; prepare the optimality-gap and scalability story.

## Recommendations
1. **Commit to the two-layer architecture now** and make it the spine of the pitch: "ML estimates, optimisation decides." Draw it as a diagram. This directly answers the PS wording "AI/ML algorithms to prioritise and schedule": explain to judges that the *prioritisation* is a criticality/risk **score** (ML) and the *scheduling* is a **constrained optimisation** (CP-SAT), which is precisely how ProRail, Trafikverket and Network Rail operate. Do not let the phrase "AI/ML algorithms" push you into making a neural network do the scheduling — that is the losing interpretation.
2. **Build the CP-SAT single-department model first (Week 4) and get it genuinely working** before touching multi-department merging or the frontend. If forced to cut scope, cut the frontend and ML sophistication first — never the optimiser.
3. **Anchor all synthetic data to published norms and lead with the CAG numbers.** Your evaluation must show a concrete asset-availability uplift over the ~50%-shortfall / 33%-unutilised baseline.
4. **Invest disproportionately in the multi-department merging model** — it is both the PS's explicit ask ("efficiently coordinating multi-department activities") and your clearest novelty.
5. **Report optimality gaps and scalability honestly.** Show the largest instance you solve, the gap achieved, and propose decomposition for national scale.
6. **Benchmarks that change the plan:**
   - If CP-SAT cannot reach a **<5–10% gap on a realistic weekly instance (one division, ~200 tasks) within a couple of minutes**, switch from fine time-indexing to interval variables and coarsen slots, or add a rolling-horizon decomposition, *before* adding any features.
   - If the ML layer is **not measurably improving decisions**, demote it to a transparent rule-based scoring function and say so openly — a defensible rule beats an opaque model that adds nothing.
   - If merging **does not increase block-hours utilised** in your evaluation, revisit the setup/withdrawal cost assumptions (they are the mechanism by which merging helps).

**Quant-career transfer note (secondary).** The core skills here transfer directly to quantitative finance: constrained optimisation and resource allocation under hard constraints (the portfolio/inventory analogue), and — most importantly — the **"ML-for-estimation feeding optimisation-for-decision" pattern**, which is exactly how alpha signals feed a constrained portfolio optimiser. CP-SAT/MILP fluency, honest optimality-gap reporting, and robust/scenario formulations are all standard quant tooling. Frame this project on your CV as "constrained resource optimisation with an ML estimation layer," not "a railway app."

- No public dataset of Indian Railways defects/blocks/possessions exists; all maintenance data must be synthesised. This is legitimate given the PS supplies none, but the synthesis must be anchored to published norms to be credible in front of railway professionals.
