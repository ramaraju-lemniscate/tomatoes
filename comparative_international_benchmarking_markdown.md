# Comparative International Benchmarking of Railway Possession/Block Planning — Design Guidance for the SIH26027 Block-Planning Optimiser

## TL;DR
- **Every high-performing infrastructure manager does one of two things India cannot copy wholesale: they either have a genuine nightly traffic-free window (Japan's Shinkansen, a ~00:00–06:00 "overnight curfew"; SBB's Lötschberg base route, Sun/Mon-night total closure 21:45–05:45), or they legally embed maintenance windows into the timetable *before* selling train paths and back that with a contractual compensation regime (Sweden's underhållsfönster, UK Schedule 4). India has neither a reliable overnight gap nor an internal compensation market — so the transferable core is the *structural discipline* (bundle work across disciplines into one possession, freeze the plan on a rolling horizon, allocate maintenance windows before train paths), not the institutional machinery.**
- **The single most transferable, evidence-backed principle is cross-disciplinary bundling into shared possession windows with setup cost amortised once.** ProRail's national programme favours nine-hour train-free periods over traditional four-hour ones; SNCF Réseau practises "massification"; DB's Generalsanierung bundles everything into one multi-month closure to buy a multi-year construction-free guarantee. Budai, Huisman & Dekker (2006) show possession cost is "mostly proportional to the track possession times," so clustering is the dominant cost lever — this maps directly onto a shared-window CP-SAT variable coordinating Engineering, S&T and Traction Distribution.
- **A credible undergraduate contribution is NOT a new algorithm (CP-SAT/MaxSAT/MILP for this problem is well-trodden — Germany 2026, ProRail 2022, Sweden 2017–2020) but the *first* integrated tri-departmental (P-Way + S&T + TD) possession coordinator built on the actual Indian systems (BDMS/TMS/SMMS/TDMS/COA) that explicitly benchmarks its constraint design against documented foreign practice. Put benchmarking in Weeks 1–2 as a design-input phase (~15–20% of effort), producing a "borrowed-features → model-constraint" matrix, not a literature essay.**

## Key Findings

1. **Two structural archetypes exist.** (a) *Traffic-free-window railways* (Japan Shinkansen, Swiss mountain base tunnels) get a physical nightly gap and simply schedule maintenance into it. (b) *Mixed-traffic European railways* (Netherlands, Sweden, UK, Germany, France) have no reliable gap and must *manufacture* windows by reserving capacity inside the timetable, compensating operators for the lost paths. India resembles (b) in traffic density but lacks (b)'s legal separation and compensation market — it is a vertically integrated manager trying to manufacture windows without a price signal for disruption.

2. **The prevalent, research-endorsed strategy is to allocate maintenance windows *before or simultaneously with* train paths, not after.** Lidén & Joborn's Swedish case study estimates integrated (joint) planning yields **11–17% maintenance cost savings** versus sequential planning (timetable-first), "without incurring any large cost increases for the train traffic." Trafikverket institutionalised this in 2015 by introducing "underhållsfönster" into the capacity-planning regime.

3. **Bundling/clustering is the dominant cost lever and is done deliberately in multiple countries** — but combining *across disciplines* in one possession is under-documented in the academic literature (most models, including Budai et al. 2006 and the 2026 German MaxSAT model, assume disciplines/projects are handled separately with known combinability), making it a legitimate novelty space for the team.

4. **Rhythmic/standardised windows outperform pure ad-hoc grant, but only if utilisation is enforced.** Sweden's cautionary result: only **34% of reserved maintenance-window capacity was used** and **68% of trackwork happened *outside* the windows** — reserving windows is necessary but not sufficient; alignment with contractor/gang needs and incentives matters.

5. **Planning horizons and freeze points are formalised most rigorously in the UK.** Network Rail runs a T-minus rolling calendar with contractually-defined freeze points and a Schedule 4 compensation regime that literally prices disruption — a ready-made template for an objective function and a rolling-horizon freeze window.

6. **Quantified optimisation-vs-manual benchmarks now exist** (Germany 2026 MaxSAT 94.62% demand coverage; ProRail 2022 hybrid beats the manual national schedule on cost and constraint violations; Sweden 11–17% savings; Germany Riedbahn full-closure cut infrastructure-caused disruptions ~27% in its first three months) — usable as comparison targets.

## Details

### 1. Country-by-country institutional practice

#### ProRail (Netherlands) — documented official practice + research
- **Framework:** ProRail defined a formal framework for scheduling maintenance possessions (referred to in the literature as ProRail, 2014). Capacity for maintenance is reserved as **train-free periods (TFP / "buitendienststelling")**, planned mostly at night on a weekly cycle. In current TTR capacity-strategy documents these appear as **TCRs (Temporary Capacity Restrictions)**; the exact start/end times of a regular TCR are fixed **23 weeks in advance**, and ProRail's stated policy is to carry out as much maintenance as possible in planned maintenance windows while planning as few work-orders as possible to preserve traffic capacity.
- **Work zones and cyclic grids:** The Netherlands divides track into working zones (den Hertog et al., 2005 — a safety-driven reform after fatal accidents) and constructs regular work-possession patterns ("single-track grids") in a **cyclic four-week plan** giving access to every part of the network at least once every four weeks. This system has been operative since 2000.
- **Planning support systems:** NS and ProRail use **DONNA** as the national capacity planning/distribution system (in development since 1999; capacity distribution depends on ~40 parties). Timetable adjustment under possession is done manually in DONNA. A widely-cited figure: the **NS team of 40 planners** starts designing adjusted timetables ~6 months ahead, and planners need about **14 weeks to generate a single feasible solution for one day of operation** (Engel, 2016, cited via Vansteenwegen et al. 2016 / van Aken et al. 2017) — the strongest single argument for automation.
- **National annual optimisation:** Oudshoorn, Koppenberg & Yorke-Smith, "Optimization of annual planned rail maintenance" (*Computer-Aided Civil and Infrastructure Engineering*, 2022; the work that informed ProRail's 2021 planning) models **>600 maintenance jobs with >8,000 scheduling options each**. A **TVP (train-free period)** counts only if ≥24 h; corridor constraints cap the number of TVPs per corridor and set minimum spacing between them. The objective mixes security/personnel/constant costs with "availability costs" (passenger/freight impediment, partly a non-monetary impediment value). The **manual ProRail schedule scored a cost of 965.2 (scaled) with 690 hard-constraint violations**; their hybrid (evolution strategy + greedy metaheuristic) produced schedules better on both cost and constraints, within a ~24 h compute budget. No clean headline "% saving" is published — do not invent one.
- **The 9h-vs-4h principle:** A ProRail/PA Consulting programme produced new principles for allocating the year's train-free periods, recommending — where night working is needed — **extended nine-hour train-free periods in preference to traditional four-hour periods**, to maximise cost efficiency, spread disruption fairly, and use scarce contractor labour fully. ProRail plans ~1,200 maintenance/construction projects a year. No quantified benefit is attached to this specific principle (qualitative only).
- **Outsourcing:** Maintenance is executed by contractors — VolkerRail, Strukton, ASSET Rail.

#### Trafikverket (Sweden) — maintenance windows inside timetabling
- **Underhållsfönster:** Trafikverket introduced "maintenance windows" into the capacity-planning regime in **2015** (Trafikverket 2015b guidance). Windows range **2–6 hours**, are set *before* the annual train timetable is finalised, and reserve roughly a tenth of line capacity. Major possessions are coordinated with international freight and published in the Network Statement as the starting point of the yearly process; minor possessions that don't affect paths are handled in a rolling weekly process.
- **Lidén & Joborn body of work:** Tomas Lidén (Linköping/VTI) and Martin Joborn built the canonical integrated planning models. Lidén (2014/2015) surveyed and classified maintenance planning (strategic/tactical/operational) and produced the widely-reused **table of maintenance time required per activity per work shift**. Lidén & Joborn (2017, *Transportation Research Part C*) presented a macroscopic MILP for integrated scheduling of maintenance windows and train services; Lidén, Kalinowski & Waterer (2018) added crew-resource considerations. The **case study (Lidén & Joborn, *Public Transport*, 2020)** on a 913 km mostly single-track northern line (82 trains/day, mostly freight) showed 2 h windows can be scheduled with small traffic adjustments and estimated that "using an integrated planning approach … instead of a sequential approach … will give maintenance cost savings of **11–17%**, without incurring any large cost increases for the train traffic"; window structure is robust unless train costs rise more than 30%.
- **Ivina & Palmqvist (2023), *Transportation Research Interdisciplinary Perspectives* 22, 100927:** empirical study of a Southern Sweden line 2019–2020. "While 10% of the total line capacity was allocated for maintenance windows, 11% of the capacity was used for maintenance. Alarmingly, **68% of trackwork occurred outside maintenance windows, and only 34% of the reserved maintenance window capacity was utilized**." Recommendations: weekend windows, windows nearer the end of the planning horizon, longer windows, and incentives to work inside windows. Their related work (Ivina et al. 2021) found trains passing trackwork are ~31% more likely to be delayed.

#### Network Rail (UK) — the most formalised horizon-and-freeze regime
- **Operational Rules:** The **Engineering Access Statement (EAS)** sets the location, number, dating and duration of possession access; the **Timetable Planning Rules (TPR)** regulate standard timings. EAS + TPR are jointly the "Operational Rules." Possessions come in classes (Section 4 / 5 / 7). **Rules of the Route / "Rules of the Resource"** capture when operators do not want access disrupted.
- **Systems:** **Possession Planning System (PPS)** is the core record of all planned/undertaken possessions; **NROL (Network Rail Online Logistics)** records planned vs actual arrival of on-track machines and possession trains.
- **T-minus timescales (from Network Code Part D and Schedule 4):** the possession "timetable" opens ~**T-30 weeks** (Draft Period Possession Plan produced at a pre-possession planning meeting ~30 weeks out); operators submit amended slot bids by **T-18**; Network Rail offers back at **T-14**; the **Confirmed Period Possession Plan (CPPP) is published 26 weeks (T-26)** before the four-week railway Period; **D-26** ("early threshold") is 26 weeks before a new working timetable comes into force (up to a year ahead for disruptive works); **T-22** is the "informed traveller" threshold feeding the **T-12** informed-traveller timetable (the first point passengers can book); a **late-change lockdown at ~T-7** is meant to freeze changes. The GHD review found the T-7 lockdown "appears to be ineffective" — the peak of worksite changes actually occurred at T-7.
- **Schedule 4 (Network Code Part D):** contractually compensates operators for planned-disruption revenue loss and cost, with **notification factors** that shrink the payout the earlier Network Rail notifies (early D-26 → informed traveller T-22 → late/applicable timetable). Its dual purpose is (a) to compensate operators, and (b) to *incentivise Network Rail to plan possessions efficiently and trade off possession length/number against disruption cost*. This is a working example of pricing disruption into planning.
- **ORR/GHD "Possessions Efficiency Review" (16 April 2021):** independent report (100+ stakeholders). Findings relevant to the team: work is "shoehorned into the timetable's white space" with efficiency/cost penalties; heavy manual spreadsheet/PDF processing is "an immense source of inefficiency"; **8.2% of trains booked to attend possessions arrived >20 minutes late**; there is no common definition or KPI set for "possession efficiency"; PPS cannot capture data in a format conducive to optimising the wider process; and the lack of a unifying location-referencing system prevents linking work-bank data to possessions. It recommended dedicated integrated long-term possession-planning teams per Route and digitalisation of the EAS.

#### Deutsche Bahn / DB InfraGO (Germany) — Baukorridore and full-closure Generalsanierung
- **Baubetriebsplanung / Baukorridore:** construction works are bundled into construction corridors.
- **Generalsanierung / Hochleistungskorridore:** since 2022, DB InfraGO shifted from incremental night/weekend possessions to **full multi-month corridor closures** — over 4,000 route-km across **41 corridors** (legal basis: the June 2024 amendment to the Federal Railway Infrastructure Development Act specifying the corridors), 2024–~2035. The **Riedbahn (Frankfurt–Mannheim, ~70 km, 300+ trains/day, capacity utilisation over 150% on some sections)** was the pilot, closed for **5 months (15 July–15 December 2024)**, during which **111 track-km, 152 switches, 130 km of overhead line and 20 stations** were renewed. Rationale: bundle *all* work into one closure so that "no major construction work is usually required for several years" (a formalised multi-year construction-free guarantee) and infrastructure-caused disruption drops sharply. **DB stated an intended reduction of infrastructure-caused disruptions "by up to 80 per cent"; the actual first-three-months result (InfraGO chief Philipp Nagl, Feb 2025) was long-distance delays down by about a third and infrastructure-caused disruptions down ~27% (up to 50% on some days), with 345 trains/day vs 329 a year earlier.** This is the sharpest real-world statement of the bundling principle: one big possession beats many small ones when setup/re-mobilisation overhead dominates. It is contested — Die Güterbahnen challenged the necessity of full closure (Bundesnetzagentur rejected the complaint 7 Feb 2025), and the programme is being stretched to ~4–5 corridors/year into the mid-2030s.
- **MaxSAT model (Reisch, Großmann & Weiß, *JRTPM* Vol. 37, 2026, art. 100569; DB InfraGO-funded):** models the whole-Germany Track Maintenance Possession Problem for one year using **predefined time-slot "containers"** whose merit is explicitly "to reduce … dependency" on timetabling; heavy machines are scheduled around optimal depots on one-week tours. On real data with **5,841 submitted demands**, "we could calculate a schedule that covers **94.62% of maintenance demands with 14 machines, which is 6% more than a MIP solver solution covers, and very close to an upper bound of 96.69%**" (MaxSAT solver Loandra vs Gurobi MIP; neither reached optimality within 24 h; problem proven NP-hard). Relevance: shows a SAT/MaxSAT route can beat MIP at national scale — but also that CP-SAT/MILP for this exact problem is already published, so novelty must lie elsewhere.

#### SNCF Réseau (France) — plages travaux and massification
- Works run in **"plages-travaux"** — reserved periods when trains don't run; in Île-de-France mostly at night, with heavy works (e.g. bridge-deck replacement) at weekends. Two types: **"stabilisées"** (recurring slots for routine maintenance) and **"spéciales"** (special slots for renewal/investment with traffic impact). SNCF Réseau explicitly practises **"massification" — regrouping works by nature and/or location** — and plans works respecting the "cadencement" (clock-face timetable). The regulator (ART, décision 2014-023; 2019-059) has repeatedly pushed SNCF Réseau to reserve works-capacity more accurately, criticised late de-programming and instability of the works plan (PGF), and urged a mechanism reflecting the *value of the capacity immobilised by works* — echoing UK Schedule 4 logic. ~30,000 staff work maintenance/works; 15–20% of routine maintenance and most heavy works are outsourced.

#### SBB (Switzerland), Japan, China
- **SBB:** Switzerland runs conventional-network maintenance largely in night windows; for the Lötschberg base route SBB publishes explicit possession windows (e.g. Sun/Mon nights total closure **21:45–05:45**; Mon/Tue full single-line working 20:45–05:00). National weekend night passenger services are a recent (2024–2026) addition and remain the exception, not the norm.
- **Japan (JR companies):** Shinkansen lines have a **structural nightly traffic-free window ~00:00–06:00** ("overnight curfew") reserved for inspection/maintenance (Doctor Yellow and other maintenance vehicles). Plasser & Theurer describe rail-exchange in narrow night windows on JR Kyushu (old-for-new material swapped within ~30 minutes using REX-S vehicles). This is India's key point of contrast: Japan gets a guaranteed daily gap by design; India does not.
- **China Railway:** high-speed lines similarly use a nightly "skylight" maintenance window (typically ~4 hours overnight) with dedicated inspection trains. *This detail could not be independently verified in this research pass; treat as indicative.*

### 2. Structural principles that could transfer

- **Bundling/clustering across disciplines.** Documented practice: DB (Generalsanierung — bundle everything into one closure), SNCF ("massification"), ProRail (9h consolidated TFPs), plus the Budai/Huisman/Dekker (2006) theoretical result that possession "costs are mostly proportional to the track possession times, which means that the preventive maintenance works should be clustered as much as possible." **Gap to exploit:** Budai et al. presume *known* combinability ("we know which routine works can be combined with each other and with which projects") and most successor models treat disciplines separately. Real practice bundles across disciplines but is under-formalised — precisely the SIH26027 tri-departmental case. Decision rule from practice: bundle when the fixed possession overhead (handover, protection setup, machine travel/mobilisation) is large relative to the marginal task time, and when tasks share the same geographic zone and time window.
- **Rhythmic/standardised windows vs ad-hoc.** ProRail cyclic 4-week grids and Sweden's fixed underhållsfönster embody rhythm; India's 2018 corridor blocks (2.5–3 h daily) and weekly 4–5 h "mega blocks" (preferably Sunday) are already rhythmic in principle. Evidence (Lidén & Joborn 2020) favours periodic integrated windows; but Ivina & Palmqvist (2023) show rhythm fails without utilisation enforcement (34% utilisation). **Implication:** the design must *measure and reward* utilisation, not just reserve slots.
- **Planning horizon and freeze points.** UK: T-30 open → T-26 CPPP → T-18/T-14 offer → ~T-7 freeze. ProRail: regular TCR times fixed at T-23; adjusted timetable ~2 months (van Aken/NS). **Trade-off:** earlier freeze = more certainty + lower compensation (UK notification factors) but less ability to respond to condition data; later = flexible but disruptive and (in the UK) more expensive. GHD's finding that the T-7 lockdown is routinely breached is a warning that freeze points must be realistic.
- **Integration with timetabling — before, simultaneous, or after.** Literature consensus and Lidén & Joborn's 11–17% saving favour *before/simultaneous*. Trafikverket allocates windows before paths; ProRail reserves TFPs then adjusts the timetable; Germany's "containers" deliberately decouple from timetabling. **This is the strongest single design principle to borrow: allocate maintenance windows as first-class citizens in the model, not as constraints bolted on after paths.** India's zero-based-timetable "maintenance corridor" (3 h/section, built into the WTT) is already a before-paths reservation — the optimiser should treat it as a hard/soft reservation to fill, not re-derive.
- **Setup/travel/withdrawal overhead and minimum useful duration.** Lidén's table gives per-activity possession-time needs; Sweden windows are 2–6 h; ProRail favours 9 h over 4 h; the Indian CAG audit found **46% of lost block time was machine travelling/shifting** and ~33% of granted block time unutilised — quantitatively identical to the overhead problem the foreign models target. **Implication:** model an explicit per-possession fixed overhead (mobilisation + protection + handover) and a minimum-useful-duration constraint; amortise the fixed overhead once per shared window.
- **How disruption cost is quantified.** UK Schedule 4 → liquidated cost + revenue-loss compensation scaled by notification lead time; ProRail → passenger impediment (degree of impediment × passengers × extra travel minutes × month multiplier × cost/minute, a partly non-monetary "impediment cost"); Sweden → delay-minutes/delay-probability. **These translate directly into objective-function terms:** a disruption penalty per possession = f(passengers/freight affected, detour/extra minutes, time-of-day, notification lead time).

### 3. Quantified outcomes and benchmarks (use as comparison targets)
- **Integrated vs sequential planning:** 11–17% maintenance cost saving (Lidén & Joborn 2020, Sweden, 913 km case).
- **Optimiser vs manual (Germany):** MaxSAT 94.62% demand coverage with 14 machines, ~6 percentage points better than MIP, vs a 96.69% upper bound (Reisch et al. 2026).
- **Optimiser vs manual (ProRail):** hybrid metaheuristic beats the manual national schedule on both cost (manual scaled cost 965.2) and hard-constraint violations (manual 690), within 24 h (Oudshoorn et al. 2022).
- **Full-closure bundling (Germany Riedbahn):** intended ≤80% cut in infrastructure-caused disruption; actual first-3-months result ~27% reduction (up to 50% on some days) and long-distance delays down ~one-third.
- **Utilisation reality check (Sweden):** 34% window utilisation, 68% of work outside windows (Ivina & Palmqvist 2023) — a benchmark of what *not* to end up with.
- **Manual effort baseline (Netherlands):** 40 planners; ~14 weeks per feasible one-day timetable under possession — the time-saving headline an automated tool can target.
- **India baseline (from the problem statement's own audits):** CAG Report 45/2017 — ~50% block shortfall (58,342 h demanded vs 29,411 h granted), ~33% of granted block time unutilised, 46% of lost block time from machine travel/shifting; CAG Report 22/2022 — ~62% of track-machine idling from block-planning failures (32% blocks not given by Operating, 30% not planned by Divisions). These are the numbers the team's before/after simulation should move.

### 4. What does NOT transfer (be explicit to judges)
- **No overnight traffic-free window.** Japan/Switzerland-style "just use the nightly gap" is unavailable on India's near-24-hour mixed-traffic trunk routes. India must *manufacture* windows inside dense traffic — so the Japanese model is an aspiration/contrast, not a template. The Indian 3 h maintenance corridor is the closest analogue and is the right unit to optimise.
- **Network scale and heterogeneity.** ProRail ~7,000 route-km vs Indian Railways ~68,000 route-km and 13,000+ daily trains. National single-shot optimisation (ProRail/Germany one-year models) may not scale directly; India likely needs decomposition (zone/division rolling horizon) — favouring CP-SAT with a rolling window over a monolithic national MILP.
- **Institutional structure / compensation.** Europe legally separates infrastructure manager and operators with contractual compensation (UK Schedule 4; French ART pushing a capacity-value mechanism). Indian Railways is vertically integrated with no internal compensation market — so the *incentive* that makes European planners minimise disruption must be *simulated inside the objective function* (a shadow price for passenger/freight delay), not assumed from contracts. This is a genuine adaptation, worth flagging as a design decision.
- **Outsourced vs in-house execution.** Netherlands/Sweden use contractors (VolkerRail, Strukton, ASSET Rail; Infranord) whose crew rest/roster and mobilisation constraints drive the models. India uses in-house departmental gangs across three departments, each with its own asset system (TMS/SMMS/TDMS). The transferable modelling object is the same (crew/gang availability + machine tours), but the three-silo data problem (BDMS coordination across TMS/SMMS/TDMS/COA) is *India-specific and is the actual novelty*.
- **Machine fleet and labour.** Different fleets and labour availability mean parameter values (task durations, machine travel speeds) must be estimated from Indian data (TMS/SMMS/TDMS) — which is exactly where the team's ML-for-parameter-estimation belongs; do not import foreign duration tables as ground truth.

### 5. The research gap — an honest assessment
- **Is there a gap?** Yes, a real but narrow one. The *optimisation methods* (MILP, CP-SAT, MaxSAT, metaheuristics) for possession/maintenance-window scheduling are mature and published for the Netherlands, Sweden, Germany, Australia and China. There is **no published, production-grade comparative study of international possession-planning practice adapted specifically to Indian Railways' three-department (P-Way/S&T/TD) coordination problem on the BDMS/TMS/SMMS/TDMS/COA stack.** Indian academic work on block optimisation is thin and mostly does not model cross-disciplinary bundling or integrate the zero-based-timetable maintenance corridor.
- **What is a real contribution (not over-claiming):**
  1. *Cross-disciplinary bundling* — a shared possession-window model coordinating Engineering + S&T + TD simultaneously, which most published models explicitly avoid (they assume projects/disciplines are separate with known combinability). This is defensible novelty.
  2. *Grounding in actual Indian systems and constraints* (maintenance corridor, mega blocks, machine-travel overhead, no compensation market → internal shadow-price objective).
  3. *A benchmarked constraint design* — every constraint traceable to documented foreign practice, with an honest "does/doesn't transfer" rationale.
- **What would be reinventing published work:** claiming CP-SAT for maintenance scheduling is novel; claiming "integrate maintenance into timetabling" is a new idea (it is the literature consensus since Lidén & Joborn 2017); claiming a national single-shot optimum. Avoid these framings.

### 6. How to structure this as a project phase (8-week build)
- **Placement and proportion:** Run benchmarking as a **Weeks 1–2 design-input phase running slightly ahead of and overlapping the first modelling sprint** — roughly **15–20% of total effort**, front-loaded, then a short **Week 6 revisit** to sanity-check the model against benchmarks. It must *inform* the model, not replace the build. Rationale: the borrowed principles are constraint-design decisions; they must land before you freeze the model structure, but you should not spend more than ~2 of 8 weeks reading.
  - Weeks 1–2: benchmarking + constraint-design matrix (in parallel with data plumbing on BDMS/TMS/SMMS/TDMS/COA).
  - Weeks 3–5: CP-SAT core build (shared-window bundling, rolling-horizon freeze, maintenance-corridor reservation, disruption objective).
  - Week 6: benchmark revisit — compare your utilisation/coverage numbers against the foreign figures; ML parameter estimation for task duration and criticality.
  - Weeks 7–8: before/after simulation vs the CAG baseline; judge-facing narrative.
- **Deliverable:** a **two-part artefact**: (1) a compact **comparison matrix** (rows = countries/IMs; columns = window type, horizon/freeze, bundling policy, integration-with-timetabling, disruption-cost mechanism, quantified outcome, transfers-to-India Y/N + why), and (2) a **"borrowed features → model constraint" table** mapping each principle to a concrete CP-SAT/MILP construct. The matrix is the evidence; the mapping table is the engineering.
- **Principle → modelling-decision mapping (the core of the deliverable):**

  | Borrowed principle (source) | Concrete CP-SAT/MILP decision |
  |---|---|
  | Cross-discipline bundling (ProRail 9h TFP; DB Generalsanierung; Budai et al.) | One shared possession-window interval variable per (section, time-block); each department's tasks are optional-interval variables that must nest inside it; fixed setup/overhead cost charged **once** per shared window (amortised), not per task |
  | Allocate windows before paths (Trafikverket 2015; Lidén & Joborn 2017; 11–17% saving) | Treat the maintenance-corridor reservation (India's 3 h/section) as a **first-class decision variable / hard reservation** the solver fills, evaluated before/with train-path feasibility, not as an afterthought |
  | Rolling horizon + freeze point (Network Rail T-26/T-7) | Rolling-horizon solve with a **freeze window**: possessions inside the freeze horizon become fixed parameters; only those beyond it are decision variables; re-solve each period |
  | Minimum useful duration + travel overhead (Lidén table; CAG 46% travel loss) | **Minimum-duration constraint** per possession; explicit **machine-travel/mobilisation time** between consecutive worksites in a machine's tour (routing sub-problem) |
  | Price disruption (UK Schedule 4; ProRail impediment cost) | Objective term = Σ possession × (passengers/freight affected × extra travel minutes × time-of-day weight); since India has no compensation market, use an **internal shadow price** for delay-minutes as the weight |
  | Utilisation enforcement (Ivina & Palmqvist 34% warning) | Add a **utilisation KPI** and penalise reserved-but-unused window capacity; report utilisation as a headline output |
  | Criticality/risk (CAG derailment link) | ML-estimated **criticality/risk score** per task feeds a weighting/priority term so safety-critical P-Way/S&T/TD work is scheduled first |

- **Framing for Indian Railways judges:** Lead with the CAG numbers (their own audit) as the problem, then: *"We studied how four national infrastructure managers with the same core conflict — maintenance vs traffic on a capacity-constrained network — actually plan possessions: ProRail (Netherlands), Trafikverket (Sweden), Network Rail (UK) and DB InfraGO (Germany). Three techniques transfer to India — cross-disciplinary bundling, allocating the maintenance corridor before train paths, and a rolling-horizon freeze — and we have mapped each to a specific constraint in our optimiser. Two do not transfer — the nightly traffic-free window (we have none) and the Schedule 4 compensation market (we are vertically integrated) — so we replaced the compensation price signal with an internal delay shadow-price in the objective. Our tool is the first to coordinate Engineering, S&T and Traction Distribution in one possession, which even the foreign models leave separate."* This reads as engineering rigour: named systems, named documents, explicit transfer/no-transfer reasoning, and a clear novelty claim that does not over-reach.

## Recommendations
1. **Do the benchmarking in Weeks 1–2 as a design input, capped at ~15–20% of effort, and deliver the two-part matrix + mapping table — not an essay.** Change-trigger: if by end of Week 2 the mapping table has fewer than ~5 concrete constraint decisions, you are reading too much and building too little — stop and start the CP-SAT core.
2. **Make the maintenance corridor (3 h/section) and mega block (4–5 h weekly) the primary optimisation units** and treat them as before-paths reservations, per Trafikverket/ProRail practice. This aligns with IR's own 2018 zero-based-timetable decision and is the lowest-risk, highest-evidence borrow.
3. **Implement cross-disciplinary bundling as the headline novelty** — one shared window variable, per-window setup cost amortised once — and say explicitly that published models (Budai et al.; German MaxSAT) leave disciplines separate.
4. **Build a disruption objective with an internal delay shadow-price** to substitute for the missing compensation market; cite UK Schedule 4 and ProRail impediment cost as the design precedent.
5. **Report utilisation as a first-class KPI** and penalise reserved-but-unused capacity, explicitly citing the Swedish 34% cautionary result.
6. **Benchmark your before/after against the CAG baseline** (50% shortfall, 33% unutilised, 46% travel loss, 62% idling from planning failures) — these are the numbers a Ministry-of-Railways judge already knows and will respect. Target: demonstrably raise utilisation and cut travel-loss in simulation.
7. **Keep ML strictly to parameter estimation** (task-duration prediction, criticality scoring), as planned — do not let ML drift into the scheduling decision, which belongs to CP-SAT. Estimate durations from TMS/SMMS/TDMS, not foreign tables.
8. **Thresholds that would change the recommendation:** if IR data shows genuine multi-hour overnight gaps on the target section, shift toward a Japan-style scheduled-window model and de-emphasise manufacturing windows; if the solver cannot handle a division in reasonable time, decompose further (section-level rolling horizon) before adding model richness.

## Caveats
